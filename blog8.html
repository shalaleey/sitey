Beyond Privacy: What AI Means for Governance and Democracy

April 25, 2026 | Saturday

The conversation between Senator Bernie Sanders and Claude presents a serious issue that reaches beyond technology and enters the areas of politics, law, economics, and public life. The discussion focuses on privacy, yet the deeper concern is governance and accountability in the age of artificial intelligence. Governance refers to the systems, institutions, and rules that guide how technologies are developed and used, while accountability concerns who is responsible when harm occurs or when systems operate unfairly or without public consent.

For many years, governments created laws and regulatory systems based on the assumption that human beings and identifiable institutions made decisions that affected society. Businesses, schools, political organizations, and government agencies were expected to explain their actions and face consequences when they violated laws or ethical standards. AI systems challenge these assumptions because they operate through large-scale data collection, automated analysis, and complex decision-making that ordinary people cannot easily understand.

In the reel, Claude explains that companies collect information from browsing history, location data, online purchases, searches, and patterns of online behavior such as how long a person pauses on a webpage. While many people already know that social media and websites gather data, the conversation highlights something more troubling: data collection is not isolated but combined into detailed profiles that predict behavior and influence decisions. These systems work quietly in the background, often hidden behind lengthy terms and conditions that people rarely read.

This creates a major governance problem because consent becomes weak or unclear. Existing legal frameworks usually assume that agreement is meaningful when people voluntarily accept terms of service, yet the reality is more complicated. Many individuals lack the time, technical knowledge, or practical choice to understand how their information is collected and shared. In this environment, consent becomes procedural rather than informed. People may technically agree while remaining unaware of what they agreed to.

AI systems like Claude do not simply store information; they rely on patterns created from enormous datasets, and those patterns can shape advertising, employment screening, loan approval, news exposure, and even political messaging. Traditional systems of accountability struggle to address this because responsibility becomes spread across multiple actors. Was it the software developer, the company collecting data, the advertiser using the system, or the platform distributing the content that caused harm?

When accountability becomes unclear, legal action and public oversight become difficult. This is why AI governance cannot be treated as a purely technical issue. The conversation between Sanders and Claude reminds people that AI is tied to power. Whoever controls data and algorithms may influence how society behaves and what information citizens encounter.

One of the strongest points raised in the reel is the connection between privacy and profit. Claude states clearly that data collection is fundamentally about money. This statement may sound simple, yet it reveals an important truth about the modern digital economy. Many online platforms and digital services appear free to users, but their business model often depends on collecting, analyzing, and selling data or using that data to attract advertisers.

AI has made this process more powerful because it can identify patterns that human analysts may never notice. Instead of merely showing general advertisements to large groups, AI systems can predict what specific individuals might buy, fear, support, or believe. This creates a new level of influence that existing governance structures were not designed to handle.

Consumer protection laws traditionally focus on fraud, false advertising, or defective products. However, AI-driven persuasion introduces subtler forms of influence. If algorithms learn a person’s emotional triggers, financial condition, or political preferences, they may present messages designed to influence decisions without the individual fully recognizing the process.

For developing countries such as the Philippines, these concerns become even more complex because governance challenges already exist before AI enters the picture. The Philippines has made progress in digital transformation, online services, and data privacy regulation, particularly through the Data Privacy Act and the work of the National Privacy Commission. However, laws alone do not guarantee protection.

Many Filipinos rely heavily on digital platforms for communication, entertainment, education, employment, and financial transactions. Social media plays a powerful role in daily life, creating opportunities for education and economic participation while also increasing exposure to surveillance, misinformation, and algorithmic influence.

Much of the AI infrastructure used in the Philippines is developed and controlled by foreign technology companies. This means data generated by Filipino users may be processed and analyzed by systems beyond the full reach of local regulators. Such dependence creates concerns about digital sovereignty and national control over data.

The rise of AI systems like Claude ultimately challenges governance because it forces societies to reconsider old assumptions about responsibility, privacy, and democratic control. Accountability in the AI era cannot depend solely on personal responsibility or consumer awareness. Governance must become more proactive and collective.

For the Philippines, this means moving beyond symbolic regulation toward practical systems of oversight and public education. Transparency, independent audits, and digital literacy programs are necessary so that citizens understand how AI affects their lives and rights.

The Sanders and Claude exchange reminds audiences that privacy is linked to power. When detailed information about millions of people becomes concentrated in corporate or political systems, the question is not merely what technology can do but who controls it and for whose benefit. If governance develops thoughtfully and responsibly, AI may support progress while protecting rights and dignity.
